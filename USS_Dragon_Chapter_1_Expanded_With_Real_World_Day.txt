CHAPTER 1 CONTINUED — THE DAY OUTSIDE

The bridge lights did not explode or flicker. They simply ceased to be part of Brian's experience. The low background sound of the ship ended. The conversations stopped. The display of the third exposure vanished, leaving the ordinary desktop on the laptop in front of him.

Brian waited a moment, making sure the connection had actually closed. A small status line read DISCONNECTED. Beneath it, the last local image file remained where he'd saved it. That mattered. The terminal was not the ship, but it was still the machine that had taken the pictures. If its screen failed again, or its capture connection went out of alignment, the next image might never make it into the card archive.

He closed the lid gently. The hinge gave the familiar little resistance he had learned to respect since the repair. He didn't slam it. He didn't leave a cable pinched under the edge. He unplugged the charger, checked the bag for the power adapter, and slipped the laptop into its sleeve.

Then he stood up in his room, and the day was just the day.

There was a dish by the sink. A receipt lay on the counter. The trash needed taking out. Nothing in the kitchen knew about the ECHO Engine. The refrigerator hummed with the indifferent confidence of an appliance that had never been asked to discover a buried pyramid.

Brian rinsed the dish. He wiped a small patch of the counter, then looked around and noticed another patch. He wiped that too. The work was ordinary and immediate. A dish was clean when it was clean. A bag of trash was heavy until it was carried outside. He liked the certainty of that, even when it was boring.

He checked his phone. There were messages to answer and errands he had put off. He replied to one, made a note about another, and set the phone down while he found his keys. For a minute he considered leaving the laptop safely at home. Then he picked up the bag. The original terminal went with him, not because the simulation was running, but because he wanted to keep it close and because he might work somewhere else later.

Outside, daylight had settled over the street. Cars passed at different speeds. A neighbor was hauling something from a trunk. Someone had left a cardboard box beside a recycling bin. The air smelled faintly of pavement and fallen leaves. Brian walked to his car, put the laptop bag where it wouldn't slide, and started the engine.

No one spoke to him from the laptop. No artificial voice interrupted the radio. The USS Dragon was not following him around as a visible starship. The session was off. That was the point.

He drove toward the store, waiting at the lights, watching a pedestrian cross with a grocery bag, noticing a bus pull away from the curb. The third exposure crossed his mind once: three photographs, the same structure in three positions. He almost reached for a way to ask Razaci about it. Then he remembered there was no active connection. He could think about the problem himself, or he could let it wait.

He let it wait until he parked.

Inside the store, a cooler door fogged briefly as he opened it. Rows of drinks stood behind the glass, their bright labels promising energy, focus, and a better afternoon. Brian reached for a can of Monster, turned it in his hand, and checked the label before adding it to his purchase. He picked up something to eat as well. At the register, the cashier asked if that was everything.

"Yeah, that's it. Thanks."

A scanner beeped. The total appeared. He paid, took the bag, and stepped aside for the next customer. Nobody asked about the ECHO Engine. Nobody knew that the man buying a drink had spent the morning studying pictures of machinery beneath imaginary pyramids in a starship simulation. The cashier had a line to move. The next customer needed milk. The world had its own schedule.

Brian sat in the car for a moment before heading home. He opened the drink, took a sip, and ate a little of what he'd bought. He didn't need to turn the errand into a mission. It was a break, a purchase, a few minutes of daylight, and a chance to return with a clearer head. He glanced at the laptop bag on the seat. The machine was closed, disconnected, quiet.

On the drive back, he stopped for fuel. He checked the receipt, cleared a few things from the passenger seat, and made another mental note about a bill. The ordinary details kept arriving. They didn't disappear just because he had built a remarkable fictional world to visit.

At home, he put the groceries away. He took out the trash. He sat for a while and finished his food. The can rested on the table beside the closed laptop. A clock advanced through the afternoon, minute by minute, without asking permission from the ship.

Only when he was ready did he clear a place to work.

He set the laptop on the desk, plugged in the charger, and opened the lid. The screen lit up to an ordinary login. No starfield. No bridge. No automatic voices. Brian entered his password and waited for the desktop to load. He checked the local capture folder first. Three files. Three timestamps. Three photographs. The third was still there.

Then he opened the remote terminal application.

The interface presented a connection status and a choice: resume the saved USS Dragon session or remain offline. Brian checked the connection details. The original imaging hardware was recognized. The repaired screen was functioning. The remote endpoint answered its handshake, but the simulation had not yet resumed.

[voice:BRIAN] "All right. Let's see what we left unfinished."

He selected RESUME SESSION.

The connection took a moment. A progress indicator advanced while the terminal verified the capture interface and restored the neurological link. Brian kept his hands still. The first sensation was not a flash of magic but the return of an environment: the familiar acoustic space of the bridge, the quiet operational hum, the awareness of a workstation in front of him.

The crew returned to work, not where the story had restarted, but exactly where it had paused. Outside the simulation, the can sat on Brian's desk, the groceries were put away, and the afternoon continued. Inside, the same unfinished problem had acquired one more useful piece of evidence.

The laptop had not carried a starship through the store. It had carried Brian's connection to one, waiting for him to choose when to return.
